# Nike FY2022 SEC EDGAR filing corpus audit

**Nike's FY2022 EDGAR filing corpus (CIK 0000320187, period June 1, 2021 through May 31, 2022) reveals ten new anomalies distinct from prior-year findings, most critically a one-year disappearance of the Section 16(a) delinquency disclosure, an undisclosed trust restructuring that preserved Knight family voting control, and Mark Parker's $81.7 million in stock sales near all-time highs with zero 10b5-1 plan disclosure—even as the SEC's proposed trading plan reforms were publicly pending.** The audit spans 12 filing categories and finds Nike maintained its pattern of governance opacity through FY2022 while simultaneously making material operational disclosures about Vietnam factory closures, Russian deconsolidation, and inflationary pressures. The following report catalogs every filing type, identifies exact accession numbers, and isolates anomalies specific to this fiscal year.

---

## Complete filing inventory and accession numbers

Nike filed its FY2022 10-K under **accession number 0000320187-22-000038** on July 21, 2022, covering the fiscal year ended May 31, 2022. The three quarterly reports carry accession numbers **0000320187-21-000045** (Q1, period ending August 31, 2021), **0000320187-22-000004** (Q2, November 30, 2021), and **0000320187-22-000017** (Q3, February 28, 2022, filed April 5, 2022). The definitive proxy statement (DEF 14A) was filed the same day as the 10-K under **accession 0000320187-22-000041**, setting a September 9, 2022 virtual annual meeting.

Approximately **10–12 8-K current reports** were filed from June 2021 through October 2022, including six quarterly earnings releases (Item 2.02), two annual meeting voting results (Item 5.07 for the October 6, 2021 and September 9, 2022 meetings), one credit facility renewal (Item 1.01, March 11, 2022), one director appointment 8-K (Item 5.02, September 20, 2022, accession 0000320187-22-000049 appointing Mónica Gil and Robert Swan), and a June 2021 material agreement (accession 0000320187-21-000020). **No 8-K/A amendments, no Item 5.03 bylaw amendments, and no Item 7.01 Regulation FD disclosures** were filed. No 10-K/A or 10-Q/A amendments were filed for any FY2022 periodic report.

On the miscellaneous filing front: **zero S-8 registration statements** (the next S-8 was filed July 2023 for ESPP shares), **zero 424B prospectus supplements** (no new debt issued; the 2020 $6 billion multi-tranche notes remained outstanding), **zero SEC comment letters or CORRESP filings** (extending the gap to approximately **13 years** since the last known correspondence in March 2009), and **zero Schedule 13D/A amendments** for Swoosh LLC (now **6 years stale** from the June 2016 amendment).

## The Section 16(a) delinquency disclosure vanishes for exactly one year

The most puzzling FY2022-specific anomaly is the treatment of the Delinquent Section 16(a) Reports disclosure. The FY2021 proxy (accession 0000320187-21-000035) **restored** this section for the first time since FY2018. The FY2022 proxy table of contents, verified directly from the SEC filing, lists only: Stock Ownership Information (p. 61), Stock Holdings of Certain Owners and Management (p. 61), Transactions with Related Persons (p. 63), Other Matters (p. 63), and Shareholder Proposals (p. 63). **No Delinquent Section 16(a) Reports heading appears anywhere in the FY2022 proxy.** The FY2023 proxy then **reinstates** this section on page 76.

This creates a one-year disappearance. Under SEC Regulation S-K Item 405, companies are encouraged to omit the caption entirely when no delinquencies occurred. If Nike had no filing delinquencies in FY2022, the omission is compliant. But the toggle pattern—present in FY2021, absent in FY2022, present again in FY2023—raises the question of whether FY2022 delinquencies existed but were not disclosed, or whether the FY2021 restoration was merely a one-off response to a specific compliance failure that was resolved. **No Form 5 annual filings were identified** for FY2022, which, combined with the omission of the delinquency section, suggests either perfect filing compliance or an undisclosed gap.

## Parker sells $81.7 million at all-time highs with zero trading plan disclosure

Mark Parker sold approximately **520,000 shares for roughly $81.7 million** during FY2022, concentrated in a five-month window from October 2021 through February 2022—precisely when NKE stock hit its **all-time high of $179.10 intraday** on November 5, 2021. The transaction detail from SEC Form 4 filings (sourced via SecForm4.com):

| Date | Shares Sold | Approximate Price | Proceeds |
|------|------------|-------------------|----------|
| Oct 13, 2021 | 100,000 | $154.99 | $15.5M |
| Nov 2, 2021 | 100,000 | $167.52 | $16.8M |
| Nov 3, 2021 | 60,000 | $171.37 | $10.3M |
| Dec 30, 2021 | 18,959 | ~$170 | $3.2M |
| Jan 7, 2022 | 81,041 | $158.37 | $12.8M |
| Jan 26, 2022 | 100,000 | $146.39 | $14.6M |
| Feb 14, 2022 | 60,000 | $141.58 | $8.5M |
| **Total** | **~520,000** | | **~$81.7M** |

**None of these Form 4 filings contained footnotes referencing a Rule 10b5-1 trading plan.** This is the fourth consecutive fiscal year of zero 10b5-1 disclosure despite cumulative insider selling now exceeding **$420 million** across FY2019–FY2022. The timing is particularly significant because the SEC published its proposed 10b5-1 amendments on **December 15, 2021** (Release 34-93782)—falling squarely between Parker's November and January sales. Despite the spotlight on trading plan reforms, neither Parker's Form 4 footnotes, the FY2022 proxy, the 10-K, any 10-Q, nor any 8-K contained any reference to "10b5-1," "trading plan," "pre-clearance," "blackout period," or "insider trading policy."

For comparison, Parker's post-2023 Form 4 filings (after the SEC's final rule took effect) explicitly reference 10b5-1 plans with specific adoption dates, confirming he was capable of using such plans all along. The FY2022 proxy CD&A discusses compensation at length but makes no mention of the insider trading framework governing NEO equity dispositions.

John Donahoe, whose $17.5 million sign-on RSUs (granted January 2020) would have begun vesting during FY2022, **did not engage in significant open-market sales** during the period, appearing primarily in Form 4 filings for grant and vesting events rather than dispositions.

## Travis Knight's quiet trust restructuring and the 6-year-stale 13D

A previously unreported governance event occurred on March 31, 2022: **Travis A. Knight resigned as trustee of the Travis A. Knight 2009 Irrevocable Trust II**, disclosed only through a voluntary Form 4 filing (accession 0001127602-22-010957, filed April 1, 2022). This was initially mischaracterized in one research source as "Philip Knight resigning from Swoosh LLC." The actual event is more nuanced and more consequential.

The Trust held **21,863,989 Class A shares directly** plus **19,142,380 Class A shares** through a managed LLC. Per the Form 4 footnotes, Travis Knight ceased reporting these ~41 million shares because he no longer had "voting or dispositive control" over them. However, Footnote 5 reveals the critical detail: **"In connection with TAK's resignation as trustee of the Trust, Holdings amended its company agreement to confirm TAK's continuing right to exercise the vote of the Class X Units of Swoosh held by Holdings."** Travis Knight therefore retained his two Class X board seats on Swoosh's five-seat board despite relinquishing the trustee role.

This structural change in the Knight family control chain—a resignation from a trust holding ~41 million Class A shares coupled with a company agreement amendment preserving Swoosh voting rights—**triggered no Schedule 13D/A amendment**. The Swoosh LLC 13D remains at Amendment No. 2, filed June 30, 2016. By the end of FY2022, this filing was **6 years stale** despite (a) Phil Knight's 2016 departure from all Swoosh roles, (b) the Travis Knight trust resignation in 2022, and (c) ongoing structural changes in the control chain. The beneficial ownership table in the FY2022 proxy reports Swoosh LLC still holding **233,500,000 Class A shares** (76.6% of class), consistent with the 2016 13D figure—suggesting no change in economic interest, even as the governance pathway was reorganized.

The triple-insider dynamic on the Swoosh board (Parker, Donahoe, and Graf holding 3 of 5 seats, with Travis Knight holding the other 2) remained intact through FY2022.

## Risk factors evolve operationally but governance remains untouched

The FY2022 10-K contains approximately **30+ individual risk factor headings across 4 categories** (Economic and Industry, Business and Operational, Global Operations, and Legal/Regulatory/Compliance). Several risk factors were materially updated or newly introduced versus FY2021:

- **Russia/Ukraine conflict**: New references to "the Russia and Ukraine conflict" in the currency and inflation risk factor, accompanied by ~$150 million in non-recurring Q4 charges for deconsolidation of Russian operations
- **Inflation**: The title of the economic risk factor was enhanced to explicitly include "fluctuations in inflation," with new language about central bank interest rate strategies and commodity cost volatility
- **NFT/cryptocurrency regulatory risk**: An entirely new risk category reflecting Nike's December 2021 acquisition of RTFKT, noting "the regulation of certain transactions we engage in, including those involving non-fungible tokens ('NFTs') and cryptocurrencies, remains in an early stage and subject to significant uncertainty"
- **Supply chain**: Expanded language on container shortages, labor disruptions, and port congestion, driven by the Vietnam factory closures
- **Hybrid work model**: New workforce risk language about "adoption of a new work model that expects employees to work on-site for a specified number of days"

**No governance-related risk factors were added in FY2022.** The 10-K contains no risk factors addressing dual-class share structure, controlling shareholder dynamics, Swoosh LLC, insider trading policy, or Section 16 compliance—identical to prior years. This absence is notable given that (a) the SEC was actively pursuing 10b5-1 reform, (b) a securities fraud lawsuit would later encompass the entire FY2022 period in its class definition, and (c) every shareholder proposal at Nike since at least 1996 has failed, arguably constituting a structural governance risk from the dual-class voting arrangement.

## Vietnam factory closures and the missing 8-K

COVID-19 Delta variant lockdowns closed Vietnamese factories beginning mid-July 2021, affecting approximately **50% of Nike footwear production** and **30% of sportswear production**. The closures lasted roughly 10 weeks, with phased reopenings starting October 2021. CFO Matt Friend disclosed on the December 2021 earnings call that **130 million units were cancelled**. Transit times doubled to approximately **80 days** from Asia to North America. Nike revised FY2022 revenue guidance from low-double-digit to mid-single-digit growth "due solely to the supply chain impacts."

This was disclosed through the Q1 FY2022 earnings press release (September 23, 2021), the Q1 10-Q, and subsequent quarterly filings. **No standalone 8-K was filed** for the factory closures despite their clearly material operational impact—a disclosure choice that routed the information through the earnings cycle rather than a current report. The FY2022 10-K later confirmed Vietnam factories manufactured **44% of Nike Brand footwear** for FY2022 (down from ~50% in FY2021), reflecting production diversification to Indonesia (30%) and China (20%).

## Board contraction, compensation reform, and ESG expansion

The board shrank from 12 to 10 directors during FY2022 through two departures: **John Connors retired** effective December 31, 2021 (served since 2005), and **Elizabeth Comstock did not stand for re-election** at the September 2022 meeting. No replacements were added during FY2022; Mónica Gil and Robert Swan were appointed only on September 20, 2022, after the annual meeting. Cathleen Benko replaced Connors on the Audit & Finance Committee effective January 1, 2022. Tim Cook was re-appointed as Lead Independent Director for a three-year term in June 2022.

Alan Graf Jr. reached his **20th year on the board** in FY2022, continuing as Audit & Finance Committee Chair at age 68. Nike's Corporate Governance Guidelines provide that "directors will not stand for re-election after reaching the age of 72," giving Graf approximately four more years of eligibility. **No term limit policy exists**, and the proxy contains no discussion of his extended tenure beyond a generic "evergreen" approach to board refreshment.

Compensation reform continued post the FY2020 say-on-pay near-failure (**~54%**). The FY2021 vote recovered to **approximately 72%**, referenced explicitly in the FY2022 proxy. Key FY2022 compensation changes included:

- **PSUs replaced cash LTIP**: Performance-based restricted stock units using Relative TSR versus S&P 500 with a "People & Planet" modifier (±20 percentage points for engagement, inclusion, leadership diversity, and sustainability)
- **Parker's compensation dramatically reduced**: Target annual direct compensation fell to approximately **$3.3 million** (down from ~$9.4 million in FY2021), with no PSP, no new PSU or LTIP awards, and only $2 million in stock options
- **Donahoe's PSP payout was 0%**: Annual performance metrics fell below threshold on a full-year basis; the Compensation Committee split the PSP into two half-year periods for non-CEO NEOs, yielding 80% payout (28% for 1H, 139% for 2H)
- **Donahoe total compensation**: $28.8 million (down from $32.9 million in FY2021)

The proxy introduced a **new dedicated section on "The Board's Role in ESG Oversight,"** enhanced director diversity disclosures with pie charts, and added a Fiscal 2022 Annual Direct Compensation Table. Parker's chairman letter explicitly noted these as FY2022 governance enhancements.

## The FY2022 annual meeting and shareholder proposal futility

The September 9, 2022 annual meeting featured five proposals. **Only one shareholder proposal** was submitted—from Domini Impact Equity Fund and Vancity Investment Management requesting Nike "adopt a policy to pause sourcing of cotton and other raw materials from China" until the U.S. government Uyghur Region business advisory is lifted. The board recommended against, and it was rejected. This was a sharp reduction from the FY2021 meeting's four shareholder proposals (political contributions, human rights, pay equity, and D&I reporting).

The exact FY2022 say-on-pay percentage was not located in available sources. However, based on the trajectory—**FY2019: ~97%, FY2020: ~54%, FY2021: ~72%, FY2023: ~97%** (per the FY2024 proxy)—the FY2022 vote likely continued the recovery trend. The shareholder proposals submitted to Nike reflect a structural futility: **every shareholder proposal at Nike since at least 1996 has been rejected**, largely because the Class A shares (controlled ~97% by the Knight family/Swoosh) elect 7 of 10 directors, and all proposals are voted by both classes combined, where Class A's numerical weight—**304.9 million shares with one vote each**—is dwarfed in economic terms but combined with management-friendly institutional holders ensures defeat.

## Institutional ownership shifts amid the stock decline

NKE's stock price traced a dramatic arc during FY2022: from approximately **$136** at the start (June 2021) to an all-time high of **~$179** in November 2021, then declining to **~$117** by May 31, 2022—a **14% full-year decline** that accelerated in the final six months. Institutional ownership changes reflected this:

- **Vanguard Group increased** from ~103 million shares (~6.7%) to ~110 million shares (~7.1%) by December 2022
- **BlackRock reduced** from ~90 million shares (~7.0%) to ~85.5 million shares (~6.8%)
- **AllianceBernstein cut significantly**, reducing by ~23.6%
- **No activist investors emerged** despite the ~40% peak-to-trough stock decline, likely because the dual-class structure makes activism structurally unwinnable

The FY2022 proxy beneficial ownership table (as of June 30, 2022) reported: **Swoosh LLC**: 233,500,000 Class A (76.6%); **Philip Knight direct**: 41,006,369 Class A (13.5%); **Travis Knight Trust**: 21,404,487 Class A (7.0%); **BlackRock**: 106,359,777 Class B (8.3%); **Vanguard**: 89,983,773 Class B (7.0%). Swoosh X Holdings, LLC holds all **300,000 shares of Preferred stock**.

## Securities fraud, buyback resumption, and the silence on governance

Three additional FY2022-specific threads merit attention. First, the **securities fraud class action** filed in 2024 (*In Re Nike, Inc. Securities Litigation*, D. Or., No. 24-cv-010974) defines a class period of **March 19, 2021 through October 1, 2024**, encompassing all of FY2022. The complaint alleges Nike and its officers misrepresented the viability of the Consumer Direct Acceleration strategy. Per reporting from The Fashion Law, Parker, Donahoe, and Friend collectively sold **1.95 million shares generating $245+ million** during the class period. No such litigation was disclosed in any FY2022 filing because the suit was not filed until 2024.

Second, Nike **resumed share repurchases aggressively** following the March 2020 suspension. FY2022 repurchases totaled **$4.0 billion (27.3 million shares)**, returning **$5.8 billion to shareholders** including $1.8 billion in dividends. The board authorized a new **$18 billion four-year repurchase program** in June 2022, announced via the Q4 earnings 8-K. By May 31, 2022, cumulative repurchases under the prior $15 billion program totaled 77.4 million shares for $8.7 billion.

Third, Nike **did not submit a comment letter** to the SEC on the December 2021 proposed 10b5-1 amendments (File No. S7-20-21). A review of the SEC's comment letter portal confirms Nike's absence from the respondent list. Nike also made **zero references** to the SEC's 10b5-1 proposal in any filing, earnings call, or proxy disclosure during FY2022—a conspicuous silence for a company whose executives were among the most active insider sellers in the S&P 500.

## Cross-reference protocol findings and synthesis of anomalies

**Compensation cross-check**: Parker's proxy SCT compensation of ~$3.3 million target versus his Form 4 sales of ~$81.7 million creates a **25:1 ratio** of equity monetization to current compensation—illustrating how the proxy CD&A understates the economic reality of Parker's annual value extraction.

**Beneficial ownership cross-check**: Swoosh's 233.5 million Class A shares in the proxy match the 2016 13D level, suggesting no change in share count despite 6 years of potential Trust/LLC structural changes.

**Insider trading policy cross-check**: A comprehensive search across every FY2022 filing type—10-K, three 10-Qs, all 8-Ks, DEF 14A, all identified Form 4s—yielded **zero references** to 10b5-1, trading plans, blackout periods, pre-clearance, or insider trading policies.

**SEC engagement check**: The ~13-year gap since the last comment letter (March 2009) extended through FY2022. No CORRESP filings, no no-action letter requests.

**Timeline integrity check**: All filing dates are internally consistent. The 10-K and DEF 14A were both filed July 21, 2022. The proxy record date (July 8, 2022) aligns with the 10-K cover page share counts.

## Conclusion

FY2022 represents a fiscal year in which Nike navigated genuine operational crises—Vietnam factory closures, Russian deconsolidation, inflationary pressures, and a 40% peak-to-trough stock decline—with substantive and timely SEC disclosure on each. The company added meaningful new risk factors (NFTs, Russia/Ukraine, inflation, hybrid work), reformed its compensation structure to address the FY2020 say-on-pay failure, and introduced ESG oversight disclosures in the proxy. These are positive governance developments.

The anomalies specific to FY2022, however, cluster around a single theme: **the governance of insider activity remains opaque.** Parker's $81.7 million in sales near all-time highs went without any 10b5-1 disclosure—the fourth consecutive year—even as the SEC was publicly proposing reforms to that exact mechanism. The Section 16(a) delinquency disclosure was dropped for exactly one year, bracketed by appearances in the FY2021 and FY2023 proxies. Travis Knight restructured his trust's relationship to Swoosh's control chain without a 13D amendment. Nike declined to comment on the SEC's insider trading proposal. And the Swoosh 13D crossed its sixth year without an update—a stale filing that obscures the current governance pathway through which the Knight family exercises voting control over a $170+ billion company.

The securities fraud lawsuit filed in 2024, with its FY2022-encompassing class period, may ultimately pressure disclosure of insider trading practices during this period. But as of the FY2022 filing corpus itself, Nike's SEC filings provide extensive operational and financial transparency while maintaining near-total silence on the insider trading framework governing its most senior executives' equity transactions.
